# 1. Capital Landscape

**Federal R&D spending (AI, security, governance):**  Federal agencies currently commit **billions** to AI and related R&D.  The government’s 2025 budget planned **$3.316 billion** in “core” AI R&D, with NSF ($494M) and NIH ($309M) as the largest civilian funders, plus DARPA ($314M) and DoD ($233M).  Additional “support” funding (e.g. cybersecurity, data management, robotics) brings total AI/IT R&D over **$4 billion**.  DARPA alone has invested **~$2 billion** since 2018 in AI advances, with a new “AI Forward” focus on trustworthy systems.  Many agencies (NSF, NIH, DOE, NIST, DHS, DOD) now have specific AI or cybersecurity initiatives.  For example, NSF recently pledged $35 million to launch the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) operations center, a public-private AI infrastructure partnership.  In sum, the federal budget picture shows **rapid growth** in AI/security funding, especially for trust, fairness, and governance research.

**SBIR/STTR (America’s Seed Fund):**  SBIR/STTR programs channel **$2–3 billion annually** into tech R&D (≈4,000 awards/year).  Phase I grants (6–12 months) range **$50K–$275K**; Phase II grants (24 months) ~$750K–$1.8M.  Dozens of agencies participate (NSF, DoD, DOE, DHS, NIH, etc.).  Many SBIR topics now explicitly invite “trustworthy AI,” privacy, authentication, cybersecurity, and blockchain solutions.  For example, NSF’s AI SBIR solicitation highlights “AI for security” and “technologies for trustworthy AI (fair, robust, explainable)”.  This makes SBIR a **major accessible channel** for QSL: companies of QSL’s size routinely apply.  However, SBIR is **highly competitive**.  (See Funding Matrix below.)

**Philanthropic funds:**  Foundations are mobilizing **tens of millions** for AI governance.  Notably, Ford Foundation’s *Humanity AI* consortium awarded ~$18M to 12 “public good” AI projects (e.g. AI Now, CDT) in 2026.  The Patrick J. McGovern Foundation pledged **$75.8M** (149 grants) for “AI for public purpose” in 2025.  Other initiatives include Google.org’s AI for Social Good grants, Ethics & Governance of AI Fund, and local efforts (e.g. Mozilla Fellowships).  These pools target open-ended research, civic tech, and policy advocacy.  While philanthropic grants are smaller individually, they often have **flexible timelines** and favor novel approaches to AI trust and equity.  QSL’s “public interest” mandate aligns well with these funders.

**State and Regional funding:**  Maryland and DC are building innovation ecosystems in AI and cybersecurity.  Maryland’s **TEDCO** operates seed and growth funds (e.g. a $1.5M Cybersecurity Investment Fund) and entrepreneurship programs.  Its *Cyber Maryland* initiative coordinates education, workforce and R&D in cybersecurity.  The state recently entered an AI partnership with Anthropic, Rockefellers and Percepta to modernize services, illustrating political support (and potential in-kind or pilot funding) for AI solutions.  DC’s local government has an active *AI Taskforce*, values guidelines, and budgets for piloting “responsible AI” tools in agencies.  While direct grant dollars are limited, these efforts create **early customer opportunities** (e.g. advising DC on its AI procurement handbook).  In short, Maryland/DC offer **public-sector innovation programs** (TEDCO, local tech councils) and early-adopter agencies that QSL can engage.

**Flow summary:**  In aggregate, QSL’s domains sit at the intersection of multiple funding streams.  Federal R&D budgets (NSF, DARPA, DOE, DHS, etc.) pour billions into AI and trust.  SBIR/STTR provides *accessible non-dilutive grants* ($50K–$1.8M) linked to agency missions.  Philanthropies are injecting tens of millions into AI governance and ethical tech.  State agencies (especially in MD/DC) support pilot projects and have innovation offices looking for solutions.  Together, this “capital landscape” suggests ample funding if QSL targets the right channels.

# 7. Risks and Uncertainties

- **Funding volatility:** Federal budgets for AI can shift with political winds.  A new Congress could cut R&D spending below projections.  QSL must avoid over-relying on one source (e.g. one agency).
- **Competition:** Many consulting firms and universities offer AI governance services or research.  Incumbents (Deloitte, IBM Trustworthy AI, large defense contractors) have deep pockets.  QSL must emphasize unique “evidence-driven” rigor.
- **Execution/Focus risk:** Pursuing grants can distract from building billable work.  QSL must guard against “proposal factory” syndrome and mission drift toward any paid work.
- **Partnership dependencies:** Tying QSL’s future to a single large partner (e.g. one big contract) could be risky if that partner’s priorities change.
- **Regulatory uncertainty:** Laws like the CLARITY Act (federal) or Digital Asset regulations could change the landscape rapidly.  While QSL’s mission is broad, sudden legal shifts might open or close some markets unexpectedly.
- **Intellectual property:** If QSL contributes to open standards or publicly funded projects, it may have limited ability to commercialize certain outcomes.
- **Trust-building time:** Government and corporate clients typically require long sales cycles.  There's a risk if QSL lacks patience for multi-year cultivating.
- **Technical viability:** Some “governance” concepts (e.g. full AI auditability) are still emerging; if regulations weaken or technologies outpace, QSL’s offerings must evolve continuously.

Managing these risks means maintaining a balanced portfolio of opportunities (see matrices above) and staying closely in touch with policy trends to pivot as needed.
